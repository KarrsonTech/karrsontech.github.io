If You Never Reach Mt. Stupid

An emotional-logic proof that P ≠ NP for humans

A. Hiker1 and The Chorus2

1Department of Wrong Turns, University of the Trailhead
2Forty people around a campfire, 2 a.m. (equal contribution, louder)

Accepted at CCEL ’26 (unanimous, sung). Not submitted to the Clay Mathematics Institute. Verification time: one scroll. Solve time: see Theorem 1.

Abstract

We resolve the P versus NP problem for the only computational substrate your manager cares about: people. Working in Emotional Logic, a proof system in which choruses are theorems, we show that the class of tasks a human can check quickly (summit photos, guitar solos, other people’s side projects) is strictly larger than the class of tasks a human can do quickly. The proof rests on four axioms drawn from a single primary source [1], a folk-metal camping song, and introduces the Helicopter Exclusion Principle: a peak reached by opinion rather than on foot shows you nothing of the other side. We also show that the Rust compiler independently agrees with us, characterize tutorial hell as verification at 2× playback speed, and present a case study in which a verdict flips sign without losing any altitude. The result has no implications for computers and several for Tuesdays.

Keywords: P vs NP, Dunning–Kruger (misapplied), helicopters, folk metal, ankles.

1Introduction

In 1971, Stephen Cook asked a question that has resisted every attempt since, including a standing one-million-dollar offer from the Clay Mathematics Institute [2, 5]. If the answer to a problem can be checked quickly, can it also be found quickly? In the notation of the field: does P = NP?

We do not answer that question. We answer a more practical one.

Every day, millions of people assert the human version of P = NP out loud. They say it in art museums (I could’ve done that), in code review (this is basically a weekend project), and in sprint planning (so that’s like two days, right?). We call this the Armchair Conjecture. In this paper we disprove it using a proof system that, as far as we know, is the first in complexity theory designed to be sung.

Our contributions:

  • Working definitions of Human-P and Human-NP (§2).
  • Four axioms, all taken from one song (§3).
  • A proof that Human-P ≠ Human-NP, including the Helicopter Exclusion Principle and a lemma checked by rustc (§4).
  • A field case study showing that Mt. Stupid is measured in certainty, not positivity (§5).
  • Several corollaries you can bring to your next estimation meeting (§6).

2Preliminaries

Definition 2.1 (Human-P). A task is in Human-P if you can do it quickly.

Definition 2.2 (Human-NP). A task is in Human-NP if, when someone hands you the finished result, you can check it quickly. Canonical members: looking at a friend’s summit photo, hearing a guitar solo and knowing it rips, glancing at someone’s side project and saying yep, that’s done. Verification time: O(1 scroll).11 Doomscrolling is O(n) scrolls and verifies nothing.

Remark 2.3. Human-P ⊆ Human-NP, as it does for computers: if you can climb it quickly, you can certainly recognize a photo of the top. All of the drama lives in the other direction.

Definition 2.4 (Mt. Stupid). A hill with a ridiculous name where your careful plans don’t count for a thing [1, Pre-Chorus]. Formally, the first local maximum of confidence as a function of miles actually walked (Figure 1).22 Figure 1 is widely attributed to Kruger and Dunning [3]. It does not appear in [3]. It is a meme. We cite it anyway, with total confidence, and invite the reader to locate us on it.

Conjecture 2.5 (The Armchair Conjecture). Human-NP ⊆ Human-P. Informally: if I can tell it’s good, I could do it.

3Axioms

All axioms are drawn from the primary source [1], reproduced in full in Appendix A. In Emotional Logic, a lyric is admissible as an axiom if it rhymes, or nearly rhymes, with something.

Axiom A1 (Trailhead Axiom). A perfect map drawn at the trailhead contains no information about your legs: I(map; legs) = 0 [6]. Evidence: the safest route keeps you parked outside [1, Verse 1].

Axiom A2 (Empirical Necessity). What you can do is measured only by trying. There is no oracle. Evidence: How will you know what you can climb? [1, Chorus].

Axiom A3 (Conservation of Stumble). Every failure is data, and data costs a sprained something. Evidence: Every clumsy step leaves a mark you can read [1, Verse 2].

Axiom A4 (Choral Lemma). A line sung once is an opinion. Sung twice, it is a conjecture. Sung three times at campfire volume, it is a theorem. Precedent: What I tell you three times is true [4].

Remark 3.1 (a careful reader objects). The final chorus rewrites its opening lines, so A2 is sung only twice and holds conjecture status. However, the closing couplet, The real summit waits beyond your doubt / You don’t get there by sitting it out, appears unchanged in all three choruses [1]. By A4 it is a theorem. We call it the Sitting-It-Out Theorem, and it is the load-bearing wall of this paper.

4Main result

Theorem 1 (Emotional P ≠ NP). Human-P ⊊ Human-NP. Equivalently, the Armchair Conjecture is false. Equivalently, the effort is very much not the same.

We first establish three lemmas.

Lemma 4.1 (Helicopter Exclusion Principle). A peak reached by opinion rather than on foot shows you nothing of the other side.

Proof. A skeptic objects: people who say I could do that are standing on Mt. Stupid, so they must have climbed something. They did not. The armchair critic occupies Mt. Stupid permanently, but arrived by helicopter (Figure 1, dotted line). Because the helicopter never lands, its passenger never descends into the Valley of Despair, and the Slope of Enlightenment is only visible from the valley floor. Having an opinion is not walking the trail. ∎

Lemma 4.2 (Human Anti-Nondeterminism). Humans possess a nondeterministic machine, but it guesses the wrong branch first, every time.

Proof. NP is defined using a nondeterministic machine that, in effect, guesses the right branch of the search tree every time. The human equivalent is documented in the primary source: Take the wrong turn, lose your footing [1, Chorus]. Since every first guess is wrong, a human has to actually walk the tree, wrong branches included. Solving therefore costs Tsolve = O(2w), where w is the number of wrong turns, while verifying still costs Tverify = O(1 scroll). ∎

Lemma 4.3 (Borrow Checker Lemma). Verifying borrows the evidence immutably. Solving requires a mutable borrow of the solver.

Proof. By compilation.

src/emotional_logic.rs/// O(1 scroll). Pure. Borrows the photo immutably.
fn verify(photo: &SummitPhoto) -> bool {
    photo.looks_legit()
}

/// O(2^wrong_turns). Needs a mutable borrow of *you*.
fn solve(you: &mut Hiker, trail: &Trail) -> Summit {
    loop {
        match you.step(trail) {
            Step::Progress => continue,
            Step::Stumble(lesson) => you.stride.absorb(lesson), // side effect
            Step::Summit(s) => return s,
        }
    }
}

/// The Armchair Conjecture, as code.
fn armchair(you: &Hiker, trail: &Trail) -> Summit {
    solve(you, trail)
}

verify and solve compile. The Armchair Conjecture does not [9]:

The side effect marked in solve is not incidental. The final chorus names it: You’ve already changed your stride [1, Final Chorus]. Verifying is a pure function. Climbing mutates the climber. ∎

Proof of Theorem 1 (by contradiction, and mild dehydration).

  1. Assume the Armchair Conjecture: whatever you can verify quickly, you can do quickly.
  2. You can verify a summit in one scroll by looking at a photo of it (Definition 2.2).
  3. Then you can reach the summit in one scroll, without leaving the parking lot.
  4. Then you know what you can climb without climbing. This contradicts A2 and, more decisively, the Sitting-It-Out Theorem, which was sung three times and is therefore true by A4.
  5. The only escape is that the parking-lot hiker is somehow already up there. By Lemma 4.1 they are on Mt. Stupid by helicopter, which does not count. By Lemma 4.2, if they started walking now, the cost would be exponential in wrong turns rather than one scroll. By Lemma 4.3, the compiler will not let them try without a mutable borrow of themselves.
  6. Contradiction. Therefore Human-P ≠ Human-NP, and since Human-P ⊆ Human-NP (Remark 2.3), the inclusion is strict. ∎

5Case study: the gallery trajectory

The following trajectory was reported to us by the first author [7]. We reproduce it without edits, because it is already a proof.

Remark 5.1 (The helicopter lands). At S1 the subject actually makes something. This is the moment the helicopter touches down, and Lemma 4.1 stops applying. Everything after S1 is real data, which is exactly why S3 hurts.

Proposition 5.2 (Sign Invariance of Mt. Stupid). Mt. Stupid is measured in certainty, not positivity. Flipping the sign of a verdict does not change its altitude.

Proof. Compare S0 (I could’ve done that) with S5 (See!? This is why you are a FAILURE). Both are delivered with maximum certainty on a sample size of one. The sign flipped. The certainty didn’t. Both sit on the summit of Mt. Stupid, facing opposite directions. ∎

Observation 5.3 (The exit). S3 is the same data in every trajectory. What decides whether S5 becomes permanent is whether the subject reads that data (A3) or rules on it. The specimen that escaped reached S6: I’ve been selling these for a fair price for a while now. Note what is missing. Genius is missing, and so is failure. It is not the highest peak, but the stride is big enough to know that, with no bad take about it. This is what calibration sounds like.

6Corollaries for productivity

Corollary 6.1 (Tutorial Hell). Watching someone build a thing is verification. Watching it at 2× speed is verification at 2× speed. Neither is in Human-P, and Lemma 4.1 applies in full: a tutorial is an excellent helicopter.

Corollary 6.2 (The Estimate). “That’s like a two-day job” is a verification-time measurement reported as a solve-time estimate. This is a units error, not a scheduling one.

Corollary 6.3 (The Demo). A demo is a summit photo. It proves the summit exists and says nothing about the trail.

Corollary 6.4 (The Kind One). Being slow at something you can instantly recognize as good is not evidence that you are bad at it. It is evidence that the task is in Human-NP, and that your taste arrived before your skill did, which is the normal order of arrival [8]. The gap between them is the trail.

7Limitations

The proof is emotional, not mathematical. P versus NP remains open, the million dollars remains unclaimed, and we ask that nobody email the Clay Mathematics Institute about this. Figure 1 is a meme (note 2). The sample size is one song, and replication with other folk-metal camping songs is encouraged. The Choral Lemma has not been validated below campfire volume. Finally, see Reviewer 2.

8Conclusion

If you never reach Mt. Stupid, you never find out what you can climb. If you reach it by helicopter, you never find out either. The only admissible proof is on foot, and it changes the one who writes it. The effort is very much not the same.

∎Quads Extremely Destroyed
